
# Humanize essays

Generated essays are smooth, balanced and empty: a broad opener, three equal body paragraphs, both sides given equal weight, a conclusion that repeats the intro. Statistically, the giveaway is low "burstiness" (sentence length and structure barely vary) and predictable word choice. A human essay takes a position, follows one thought further than expected, and sounds like someone. Also read `references/core-patterns.md` and `references/word-list.md`.

## Integrity

If the essay is for school, a scholarship or a contest, the rules about AI help are the user's to follow. This skill edits and improves; it doesn't manufacture authorship. **Never invent personal stories, feelings, people, places, quotes or sources.** Ask the writer for a real detail instead: that's what makes an essay theirs, and it's the single biggest improvement you can make.

## Tells and fixes

| Tell | Fix |
|---|---|
| ★ Broad opener: "Throughout history…", "In today's fast-paced world…", "Since the dawn of time…", a dictionary definition | Start in the specific: a moment, a claim, a question the essay answers |
| ★ "In conclusion," / "In summary," + restated thesis | End on the furthest point the essay reached, or an implication. Cut the recap |
| Five-paragraph symmetry (intro, 3 equal bodies, conclusion) | Let the argument set the shape. One point may need three paragraphs; another, two sentences |
| "On one hand… on the other hand…" with no verdict | Take the position the writer holds; address the strongest objection directly |
| Thesis as triad ("X impacts the economy, society, and the environment") | The actual, arguable claim |
| Topic sentences that announce ("Another important aspect is…") | Topic sentences that claim |
| "This shows that…" / "This highlights the importance of…" after every example | Cut; or say what's surprising about the example |
| Manufactured contrast ("It's not about X; it's about Y") | Say Y |
| One-line slogan paragraphs ("And that makes all the difference.") | Delete |
| Uniform sentence length | Mix: a four-word sentence after a long one lands harder |
| Rhetorical-question chains ("But what does this mean? Why does it matter?") | One question at most, if the essay answers it |
| Generic examples ("For example, many people use social media") | A specific, real example the writer knows or the source provides |
| AI vocabulary cluster (delve, tapestry, multifaceted, pivotal, underscore) | Plain words |
| Headings and bullets inside an essay | Prose with transitions (unless it's a blog post that uses them) |

## Personal statements

- Admissions readers say generic statements blur together. The fix is a concrete scene and honest reflection only the writer could give.
- Cut "passion for," "ever since I was young," "this experience taught me the importance of."
- Show the change through what the writer did differently, not adjectives.
- Keep word limits exactly.

## Blog posts and op-eds

- Hook = the most interesting specific thing, not a setup.
- Short paragraphs are fine; slogan paragraphs aren't.
- Have one opinion and defend it. Cut the "it depends" ending unless it really does.

## Voice

If the user provides prior writing, copy its habits: average sentence length, contractions, humor, punctuation, favorite transitions, how they open paragraphs. Their quirks are features. Don't sand off a writer's slightly odd but deliberate phrasing.

## Workflow

1. Write the essay's real claim in one sentence. If it has none, ask the writer what they think.
2. Fix structure: opener, paragraph shape, ending.
3. Rewrite paragraph by paragraph for rhythm and specificity.
4. Mark places where a real detail would help, and ask for it rather than inventing it.
5. Check quotes, citations, facts and word count against the original.
